CHAPTER 1

The Indian National Movement

The First War of Independence
(The Great Uprising of 1857)

The First War of Independence, also known as the Revolt of 1857, marks a pivotal chapter in Indian history. This uprising against British colonial rule was sparked by widespread discontent with economic exploitation, political annexation, and cultural suppression. Often described as a spontaneous rebellion, it was fueled by the cumulative grievances of various segments of Indian society, including soldiers, peasants, and princes. While the revolt ultimately failed to achieve its immediate objectives, it symbolised a significant awakening of national consciousness and set the stage for future struggles for independence. The events of 1857 serve as a testament to the resilience and enduring spirit of resistance among the Indian people.

Though it started as a Mutiny of Sepoys at Meerut on 10th May 1857, the Revolt soon engulfed the whole of North India and shook the very foundation of the British rule in India. But it was not the first such revolt. From time to time different groups of Indians – farmers, tribal groups and soldiers – had risen in revolt against their ruthless oppression by the British. But unlike the 1857 Revolt, most of these uprisings were localised and were easily suppressed by the British.

CAUSES OF THE GREAT UPRISING OF 1857

The Great Uprising of 1857 was not the result of any one particular cause. To understand these causes we have to study the history of the preceding century. The reason is that these causes were as old as the British Rule in India.

I. Political Causes

Many political causes antagonised the Indian rulers against the British Rule:

(1) Subsidiary Alliance : The Subsidiary Alliance was a strategic policy introduced by Lord Wellesley, the Governor-General of India, during his tenure from 1798 to 1805. This policy played a crucial role in the expansion and consolidation of British power in India. Indian rulers had to disband their own military forces. They were required to maintain British troops within their territories.

FACT FILE

The Great Uprising of 1857 was not the First Revolt against the British. Between 1763 and 1856 there were forty major rebellions in different parts of India.

• The Sanyasis or Fakirs of Bengal organised peasants into an army of 50,000 soldiers and harassed the British for 30 years.
• The Zamindars of Ramanathapuram and Shivganga revolted in Travancore in 1808–29 (called the Polygar rebellion).
• Rani Chennamma of Kittur led a revolt during 1824–29.
• Soldiers and peasants revolted in Rangpur, Devapur, Birbhum, Bishnupur and Sambalpur in the eastern districts.
• A military uprising of sepoys in Vellore (1806).
• Besides these, tribals like Bhils, Kols, Khasis and Santhals of Madhya Pradesh, Bihar also revolted between 1815–1856.
• Unlike the Great Uprising of 1857, these uprisings were localised. The British suppressed them easily.

A British Resident was appointed at the court of the Indian ruler. Indian states were forbidden from entering alliances with other foreign powers or waging wars without British consent. The cost of maintaining the British troops was borne by the Indian states, often placing a heavy financial burden on them. In return, the British promised to protect the Indian states from external threats and internal revolts, effectively making these states dependent on British military support. The policy significantly eroded the autonomy of Indian states, as they could no longer make independent political or military decisions.

(2) Doctrine of Lapse : Lord Dalhousie; the Governor-General from 1848 to 1856 introduced Doctrine of Lapse. The Doctrine of Lapse was a policy implemented by the British East India Company during the mid-19th century. Under this doctrine, if a ruling Indian prince died without a natural heir, his kingdom would be annexed by the British. This policy led to the annexation of several states, including Satara, Jhansi, and Nagpur. The Doctrine of Lapse caused widespread resentment among Indian rulers and played a significant role in fueling the discontent that led to the Revolt of 1857.

[Image/Diagram: Portrait of Lord Dalhousie]

Lord Dalhousie

(3) Disrespect Shown to Bahadur Shah : The House of the Mughals was also humbled when the English announced that after the death of Bahadur Shah II, the Mughals would lose the title of kings and his successors would have to vacate the Red Fort. It has been rightly remarked that the increase in the disrespect shown by the English to the Emperor, increased the aversion of the Indians for the British.

[Image/Diagram: Portrait of Bahadur Shah Zafar]

Bahadur Shah Zafar

(4) Ill-Treatment of Nana Sahib and Rani of Jhansi : Baji Rao II, the last Peshwa, adopted Nana Sahib as his son. After the death of the Peshwa, the English refused to acknowledge Nana Sahib as the ruler of the Marathas. The Rani of Jhansi, Laxmi Bai, was also not allowed to adopt a son. They became sworn enemies of the English. This policy created resentment among the Indians.

(5) Annexation of Awadh : In 1856, Lord Dalhousie annexed Awadh to the Company’s dominions on the pretext of alleged misrule or misgovernance. On 7 February, 1856 Nawab Wazid Ali Shah was deposed and on February 13, the Court of Directors ordered

complete annexation of Awadh. The annexation of Awadh was certainly a case of high-handedness on the part of the Company. About Awadh, Lord Dalhousie had predicted that it was a “Cherry that will drop into our mouth one day.” The British seemed to have broken promises and pledges made to the ruling chiefs. This caused resentment among the Indians, particularly Awadh.

• They had to face more hardships.
• They had to pay higher land revenue and additional taxes on food, houses, ferries and justice.

[Image/Diagram: Portrait of Wazid Ali Shah]

The last Nawab of Awadh — Wazid Ali Shah

• The dissolution of the Nawab of Awadh’s army and administration threw thousands of nobles, officials and soldiers out of jobs.
• The British confiscated the estates of the taluqdars or zamindars, who became opponents of the British rule.
• The Company’s sepoys were worst affected. They did not like their homelands coming under foreign rule. It also affected their financial position. They had to pay higher taxes on the land their families held in Awadh.

(6) Absentee Sovereignty of the British: Absentee Sovereignty means that India was being ruled by the British Government from England, at a distance of thousands of miles. Absentee Sovereignty of the British rule was resented by the Indians. The earlier rulers like the Mughals, who had conquered India, had in course of time, settled in India. The revenues they collected from the Indians were spent in India only. But in the case of Britain, the Indians felt that they were being ruled from England and India’s wealth was being drained to England and not utilised for their welfare.

(7) Other Factors

• The British declined to honour the pledges and treaties made with native princes.
• Indians were treated with contempt and they were excluded from every sphere of the administration.
• With the defeat of the British Army in the first Afghan war, the myth of British supremacy was shattered.
• The vague and floating rumours that the British came to power in 1757 (after the Battle of Plassey) and their rule would come to an end in 1857, added fuel to the fire. So people believed that the time had come for the British rule to end.

II. Social and Religious Causes

The British government’s attempt to interfere in the social and religious life of the Indians led to the widespread fear among the masses.

(1) Interference with Social Customs: The British openly interfered with the social and religious customs of the Indians. Ignoring the sentiments of the people, the government passed laws aiming at improving the conditions of people.

• The 1829 Act abolishing Sati was regarded as an undue interference in the religion of the Indians.
• The two laws of 1832 and 1850, which permitted the Christian converts to inherit property, became very unpopular.
• The Widow Remarriage Act of 1856 offended the Indians, and it was regarded as an infringement on their religious sentiments.

(2) Activities of Missionaries:

• The British encouraged the Christian missionaries from Europe to spread Christianity in India and to translate the Bible into the Indian languages.
• These missionaries often condemned Hindu and Muslim customs as outdated, hurting the sentiments of both the communities.

• The Missionaries opened schools to spread English education where the Christian religion was also taught.
• The religious sentiments of the people were further hurt when a tax was imposed on property held by temples and mosques.

(3) The Expansion of Telegraph Lines and Introduction of Railways : People were suspicious of introduction of modern innovations like railways and telegraphs. There were rumours that telegraph poles were erected to hang those who were against the British rule. Similarly, orthodox Indians noted that in the railway compartments the higher castes and the lower castes were made to sit side by side.

(4) Resistance to Western Education and the Preservation of Traditional Learning : The shifting of emphasis from oriental learning to western education was not received well by the people, especially the Pandits and the Maulvis. They saw it an attempt to discourage traditional Islamic and Hindu studies. People started suspecting that the aim of western education was not to promote literature and sciences but to encourage children to become Christians.

(5) Impact of Racial Discrimination by the British on Indian Society : The policy of racial discrimination made the English believe that they belonged to a ruling class and had a feeling of superiority. They looked down upon Indians and treated them with arrogance and often insulted them. Such acts of unjust discrimination alienated the British from the Indian masses.

III. Economic Causes

The economic policies of the British resulted in impoverishing all the segments of Indian society, except a handful of collaborators among the Indians.

(1) Exploitation of Economic Resources of India : The East India Company reduced agricultural India to an economic colony to serve the interests of industrial England. The Company purchased textiles, indigo, spices and tea from India and sold them abroad at exorbitant prices. By buying and exporting Indian goods, the Company encouraged their production in India. But gradually Indian textiles began to compete with the textiles manufactured in England. The British put pressure on the East India Company to forbid the sale of Indian textiles in England. It put restriction on the import of Indian textiles imposing heavy import duties on such goods.

(2) Subordination of Indian Industry : After the Industrial Revolution, England developed its own textile industry. This affected the textile industry in India. Goods manufactured in India were subjected to high import duties in England. Secondly, the Company purchased raw cotton and jute from India at cheaper rate for factories in England. Soon, India became a country supplying raw materials to England. The British manufactured finished goods from Indian raw materials and exported them back to India making enormous profit.

Gradually, India became an economic colony of Industrial England. India became an importer of British made goods. On the other hand, export of raw materials and foodgrains deprived India of her agricultural surplus and raised the prices of raw materials. Thus, the change in the nature of India’s trade became an instrument for exploiting India’s resources.

(3) Collapse of Indian Handicrafts : As a result of the British policy of making India a raw material producing country, Indian handicrafts and cottage industries died out. Important industrial towns such as Dhaka and Surat declined. Heavy duties on Indian silk and cotton textiles in Britain destroyed Indian industries. Thousands of craftsmen and artisans were thrown out of employment. The manufacturers of silk and cotton goods got no profits from their work and began to look for other means of livelihood. The misery of the artisans was further compounded by the disappearance of their traditional patrons and buyers — the princes, chieftains and zamindars.

(4) Drain of Wealth : The transfer of wealth from India to England for which India got no proportionate economic return is called the drain of wealth. Till the Battle of Plassey (1757) the European traders used to bring gold into India to buy Indian cotton and silk. However, after the conquest of Bengal, the British stopped sending gold to India.

They began to purchase raw materials for their industries in England from the surplus revenues of Bengal and profits from duty free inland trade. Thus, began the process of plundering India’s raw materials, resources and wealth by Britain.

The drain also included the salaries, incomes and savings of Englishmen, the British expenditure in India on the purchase of military goods, office establishments, interest on debts, unnecessary expenditure on the army, etc.

Regarding the British exploitation, John Sullivan, President of the Board of Revenue, Madras, aptly remarked, “Our system acts very much like a sponge, drawing up all good things from the banks of the Ganges, and squeezing them down on the banks of the Thames.”

(5) Unemployment Among the People : Due to Dalhousie’s policy of indiscriminate annexation of the native states, many Indians lost their jobs. After the annexation of Oudh the army was disbanded. The soldiers lost their livelihood. They turned out to be bitter enemies of the foreign rule in India. They became the avowed enemies of the English and joined the revolt.

Educated Indians became jobless. They were excluded from high posts. Their lot became miserable and they felt discontented.

(6) Resumption of Tax-Free Lands : The resumption of tax-free land and confiscation of Jagirs caused discontent among the Jagirdars and Zamindars. Lord William Bentinck had attached several Jagirs in Bengal. As a result, the Indian aristocracy was reduced to utter poverty. Lord Dalhousie had also confiscated Jagirs belonging to Jagirdars of the Deccan region. They became staunch enemies of English and the leaders of the uprising.

(7) Poverty and Famine : With the collapse of the Indian economy, there was widespread poverty among the masses. In addition to this, there were severe famines which crippled the life and economy of people. They were so frustrated with their economic condition that they thought their lot would improve only after the British left India.

(8) Economic Decline of Peasantry : The peasants were discontent with the official land revenue policy and the consequent loss of their land. In fact, it was the peasantry that bore the heavy burden of taxes to provide money for the trade of the Company, the cost of administration and the wars of British expansion in India. For example, in Bengal, the land revenue was double the amount collected under the Mughals. Not even a part of this revenue was spent on the development of agriculture or welfare of the cultivators. Increase in land revenue forced many peasants into indebtedness. The traditional zamindars were replaced by merchants and moneylenders. These new landowners had no concern for the peasants. They pushed rents to exorbitant levels and evicted their tenants in case of non-payment. The economic decline of the peasants affected cultivation and led to many famines.

IV. Military Causes : Discontent among Indian Soldiers

The above causes had prepared the ground for a rising but it could not erupt as long as the Indian soldiers were loyal to the English. But there were several causes that roused discontentment among the sepoys as well, who started the uprising.

(1) Ill-Treatment of Indian Sepoys : There was no fellow feelings between the English and the Indian soldiers. The Indian soldiers were considered inferior to the English soldiers. They helped the English to establish their empire in India but they were treated with contempt by the British officers. During training they were abused and kicked as if they were animals. Such treatment broke their hearts and hurt their feelings. British military authorities forbade sepoys from wearing caste or sectarian marks, beards or turbans.

[Image/Diagram: Indian Sepoys]

Indian Sepoys

(2) Inferior Status and Low Salaries : The wages of the Indian soldiers were too less to support their families. An English soldier was paid more than 8 times an Indian sepoy while their duties were of a similar nature. The Indian had no chance of promotion either. They were not appointed to high posts in the army. No Indian could rise higher than the post of Subedar and his salary too was less than that of an English soldier. In fact, the sepoy’s life was very hard and he resented his inferior position.

(3) General Service Enlistment Act (1856) : Lord Canning passed the General Service Enlistment Act. It created bitterness among the sepoys as it bound them to be sent to fight even to an overseas front. The sepoys opposed this Act because it was contrary to their religious beliefs. The Act did not take into account the sentiments of Indian soldiers. The Brahmin soldiers saw in this a danger to their caste. This led to a feeling of resentment among them.

(4) Loss of British Prestige in the First Afghan War : A myth prevailed among the soldiers that the English were invincible. But the beating they received in the First Afghan War broke this myth and the Indian sepoys felt that the British could also be defeated. So they gathered courage to take up arms against them.

The Crimean War (1853-56) broke out between England and Russia in Europe. A large number of European soldiers were pulled back to Europe. The fall in the number of European troops in India encouraged the Indian sepoys to break out in an open revolt.

(5) Effect of the Annexation of Oudh : The Bengal Army was very strong and well-armed. It consisted mainly of the soldiers drawn from Oudh. The Annexation of Oudh in 1856 sent a wave of resentment among them and inflamed their feelings.

(6) Numerical Strength of the Indian Soldiers : The number of Indian soldiers was five times that of the British troops. It encouraged the Indian soldiers to take up arms against the English.

V. Immediate Cause : Greased Cartridges

By 1857, the material for a mass uprising was ready. Only a spark was needed to set it on fire. It was triggered by the greased cartridges. A new type of rifle (Enfield Rifle) was introduced in the army. Greased cartridges were to be used in them. Before fitting them in the rifles, the soldiers had to bite the tip of the cartridges with their teeth. A rumour spread that they were greased with the fat of the cow and the pig. Both the Hindu and the Muslim sepoys were enraged and thought that the English Government wanted to defile their religions. Both refused to use these cartridges and when force was used against them, they became infuriated and rose in revolt.

BEGINNING OF THE WAR

The rumour about the greased cartridges spread like wildfire. The Great Uprising started from Barrackpore in Bengal. A Brahmin sepoy, named Mangal Pandey, refused to use the greased cartridges (29 March, 1857) and killed two English officers. He was hanged on 8 April, 1857. For fellow sepoys he became a martyr. This resulted soon in general uprising in Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, etc.

MAIN EVENTS

1. Meerut : The real trouble started at Meerut on May, 6, 1857. It gathered momentum and spread across Northern India like a wild fire. About 86 sepoys refused to use the greased cartridges.

They were dismissed and sentenced to 10 years imprisonment. On 10 May, 1857 their companions revolted, killed the officers, attacked the jail and released the prisoners.

[Image/Diagram: Main centres of Revolt 1857 map]

Main Centres of the Revolt of 1857

2. Delhi : The Meerut soldiers set fire to the bungalows of the English and set off for Delhi. They reached Delhi early morning on 11 May. The local infantry joined them. They killed their officers and captured the city. The last Mughal King, Bahadur Shah II, was declared the Emperor of India. The soldiers from other areas also poured into Delhi. Soon the mutiny of the sepoys changed into a revolutionary war. But the success of the revolutionaries proved short-lived. Soon, Sir John Nicholson besieged Delhi and he, ultimately, succeeded in capturing Delhi in September, 1857. Thousands of soldiers and people were put to death mercilessly under the supervision of General Neil. Bahadur Shah was caught, sentenced to life imprisonment and deported to Rangoon where he died in 1862. Both his sons were shot dead before his very eyes. Thus, the mighty Mughal Empire came to an end.

Though aged and powerless, Bahadur Shah became the symbol of India’s political unity. He became a rallying point of all those who wanted to put an end to the foreign rule. That is why rebellious soldiers from all over the country turned their steps towards Delhi.

Likewise, all the Indian chiefs who took part in the Great Uprising proclaimed their loyalty to Bahadur Shah. He wrote letters to different rulers asking them to unite and form a confederacy to drive the British out. Thus, Bahadur Shah acted as a unifying force. This was his great contribution.

[Image/Diagram: Capture of Bahadur Shah Zafar and his sons at Humayun’s Tomb on 20 September 1857]

3. Kanpur : At Kanpur, the Great Uprising was led by Nana Sahib, the adopted son of Peshwa Baji Rao II. He was a brave and determined general. He captured the fort and proclaimed himself the Peshwa. He showed signs of a national hero when he acknowledged Bahadur Shah as the Emperor of India. This very fact greatly cemented the Hindu-Muslim unity which was the need of the hour. With the help of Tantia Tope and Azimullah he gave some tough moments to the British who were forced to announce a reward of rupees one lakh for the capture of Nana Sahib. At last, General Havelock arrived on the scene with a large force. Because of a larger army and better equipment he defeated Nana Sahib and recaptured the city. Nana Sahib fled and was not heard of again. The chief burden of fighting now fell on his capable and trusted general, Tantia Tope. He seized the city again but the English succeeded in reoccupying it.

5. Benaras and Allahabad : The news of the uprising reached Benaras. The revolutionaries revolted and killed many English soldiers and officers. General Neil reached there. He committed inhuman atrocities on the revolutionaries and crushed the uprising. There was a rising at Allahabad also in June under the leadership of Maulvi Liaquat Ali. The rebels succeeded in capturing the fort and the city of Allahabad. General Neil arrived on the scene and put down the rebellion by his harsh policy.

6. Bihar : Kunwar Singh organised this movement in Bihar because his small kingdom of Jagdishpur had been annexed by the English. He extended his operation to Rewa, Banda and Kalpi and also helped Nana Sahib. He died after a gallant fight on April 27, 1858. His brother Amar Singh took the command of the rebels after his death. All his companions were killed in one of the battles and he was forced to run away. The English were able to crush the revolt in Bihar.

7. Central India : The rebels in Central India were led by a brave and patriotic lady, Rani Laxmi Bai of Jhansi. She was joined by the valiant general, Tantia Tope. The Rani fought like a true heroine. Her courage and military skill inspired her countrymen. She fought gallantly but Jhansi was captured by the English with the help of some traitors. The Rani fled towards Kalpi. English general, Sir Hugh Rose, followed her. But Rani Laxmi Bai did not lose heart. Seldom was shown such a valour and determination as was shown by this great heroine of the national movement. Along with Tantia Tope she fell on Gwalior like a lioness and seized it from Scindia who was loyal to the English. Scindia had to take refuge with the English at Agra.

The great sacrifice of Rani Jhansi is still remembered by every Indian. She would ever be a source of great inspiration for all of us.

RESULTS/CONSEQUENCES OF THE GREAT UPRISING

Though the Uprising of 1857 failed and was firmly suppressed, yet it proved very important in respect of its results. It produced deep and far-reaching effects on the social and political life of India. According to Sir Griffins, “Perhaps a more fortunate occurrence than the Mutiny of 1857 never occurred in India. It swept the Indian sky clear of many clouds.”

Results Pertaining to Constitutional and Political Field

(1) End of the Company’s Rule : The first and most important result of the Uprising was that the hundred years old Company’s Rule in India came to an end. The Board of Control was abolished and the Board of Directors was stripped of all it powers. Now the Government of India passed into the hands of the British Queen and the Parliament, who appointed a Secretary of State for India to look after the administration of India.

[Image/Diagram: Portrait of Queen Victoria]

From 1858 Queen Victoria became the ruler of India

The Secretary of State of India was a member of the British Cabinet. A 15 member Council, eight of whose members were appointed by the Queen and seven by the Directors of the Company, was constituted to aid and advise the Secretary of State. His office was in London but all his expenditures were met by the Indian Government.

(2) Change in the Designation of the Governor-General : With the transfer of the Government from the hands of the Company to those of the Crown, the designation of the Governor-General was also changed. While he remained the Governor-General for the provinces under his rule, he came to be known as the Viceroy while dealing with the Nawabs and the Rajas and other native princes.

(3) Accepting the Previous Treaties : The British Government fully realised that the Great Uprising had broken out mainly due to the discontent caused by their aggressive policies towards the Indian princes. Therefore they took various measures to appease the princes for making them loyal to the British. The first of these measures was to honour all the treaties entered by the Indian rulers with the East India Company. In this way they tried to win back the trust of the princes.

(4) Change in Policy towards the Indian States : To win back the confidence of the native princes the most important measure taken by the English was to switch over to the policy of subordinate union instead of the earlier policy of subordinate isolation.

• Indian princes were assured that their kingdoms would never be annexed.
• The Doctrine of Lapse was given a final goodbye and the Indian rulers were allowed the right of adoption.
• But the number of Indian soldiers in their armies was restricted and they were forbidden to enter into any relations with any native or foreign power without the consent of the British Government.
• They were also assured that there would be no interference in their internal matters except in case of misgovernance.
• All these princes who had helped the British during this Uprising were decorated with big honours and rewarded fabulously to seek their appeasement. The Nizam of Hyderabad was relieved of his debt of 5,00,000 pounds.

(5) Abolition of Peshwaship and the Mughal Line : The adopted son of Peshwa Baji Rao II — Nana Sahib — had taken an active part in the Uprising of 1857 but he had voluntarily escaped into Nepal after the failure of the Great Uprising without any intention

no return. The office of the Peshwa was abolished. With the abolition of Peshwaship one of the chief worries of the English ended. In the same way the title of the Mughal Emperor was abolished following the death of Bahadur Shah II, who had been actively associated with the Great Uprising. Thus, ended the line of the Great Mughals founded by Babur in 1526.

(6) Policy of Divide and Rule : During this revolution there were ample instances of the Hindu-Muslim unity and also of the people supporting their princes against the British. Such a thing was considered harmful to the imperialistic designs of the English who would not shirk even to take questionable measures to sabotage this unity by following a policy of ‘Divide and Rule’. They alienated the rulers from their subjects by according them special protections and by allowing them several concessions. Thus, the Indian rulers were led to the ruinous path of luxurious life and they began to disregard the vital interests of their people. The British also encouraged hatred and ill-feeling among the various sections, especially the Hindus and the Muslims, so that they would never unite again to pose a threat to the British Rule in India.

(7) Rise of Nationalism : A far-reaching effect of the Uprising of 1857 was that the sacrifices of the Indian leaders gave birth to the nationalist movement.

Results Pertaining to the Army : Reorganisation

(1) Amalgamation of Troops of the Crown and the Company : Since 1784 when Pitt’s India Act was passed, the Company had two categories of European troops — one that were recruited from among the European settlers in India by the Company itself and the other were those recruited directly by the British Government in England and sent to serve in India.

(2) Increase in the Number of European Troops : At the time of the Great Uprising the number of European soldiers was far less than the number of Indian troops. It had been a factor that encouraged the Indian sepoys to break out in open revolt. After the Uprising it was laid as a principle that in no case the number of the Indian sepoys would exceed twice that of the European troops. The new army consisted of about 72,000 Europeans and 1,35,000 Indian troops.

(3) Placing Artillery and Other Effective Weapons Exclusively in European Hands : After the Uprising, Indian officers were never placed in charge of ammunition, nor were they allowed to use more sophisticated weapons of war. It was done primarily to desist the Indian soldiers from harbouring any design of breaking out in rebellion.

(4) Mixing of Different Classes of Sepoys : The soldiers of the Bengal army belonging to the same caste and practising same faith had played an important role in provoking the Uprising. Therefore, it was decided to mix the soldiers belonging to different castes and practising different faiths in the same armies, so that unity and nationalism did not develop among them. Besides, the Indian sepoys were stationed in cantonments away from the civil areas where they would not come in contact with the common people.

(5) Increase in Expenditure on the Army : A European soldier got salary 4 to 5 times more than an Indian sepoy. It was, therefore, natural that an increase in the number of European soldiers resulted in a proportionate increase in the expenditure.

Results Pertaining to the Religious, Social and Economic Fields

(1) Promise of Religious Freedom : It was clearly stated in the Queen’s Declaration of 1858 that there would be no interference in the religious beliefs and practices of the people and all would enjoy full freedom in this respect.

(2) Promise of Free and Impartial Admittance to Service : The Indians were promised appointment to the highest offices under the Government without prejudice to caste, colour or religion. It was particularly stated in the Declaration of 1858.

(3) Straining of Relations between the Europeans and the Indians : Both the Indians and the Europeans committed such atrocities against each other that the relations among the people of both the races became bitter and strained. The Indians hated the British while the British suspected the Indians.

(4) Widening of the Gulf between the Hindus and the Muslims : Both the Hindus and the Muslims had showed utmost zeal and enthusiasm in this Uprising. In later years the English made capital out of the

differences and fanned the Hindu-Muslim mistrust so that the two communities might never come together and thus the British Empire would stay longer in India without any challenge from any quarter whatsoever.

(5) Economic Exploitation : The wealth of India had been plundered by the Company, but with the introduction of the Crown’s Rule it became more serious and much wider.

THE LESSON AT A GLANCE

Causes of the Great Uprising of 1857

I. Political Causes
• The Doctrine of Lapse had caused Indian territories to be annexed by the English and this angered the Indians.
• Disrespect shown to Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah was intolerable for the Indians.
• Ill-treatment of Nana Sahib and Rani of Jhansi, annexation of Oudh and disbanding of armies of annexed states agitated the people and soldiers who wanted to revenge.
• Other princes were suspicious of the English and had observed how the Indians were ill-treated. Their arrogance and insults made the princes angry. They wanted to get rid of the English.

II. Social and Religious Causes
• The English adopted forceful and violent methods to spread Christianity. They insulted Islam and Hinduism. When the English abolished Sati the Hindus saw this as an interference in their religious affairs.
• The Brahmins and the Maulvis were upset when western culture was introduced and their role and influence in society declined. Fear and suspicion was aroused by the introduction of telegraphs and railways.
• Tax levied on religious sites and the insulting attitude of the English added fuel to fire. Unrest and discontent was rising in the hearts of the Indians.

III. Economic Causes
• Exploiting economic resources and subordinating Indian economy to the interests of British trade and industry led to the decline of Indian industry.
• Handicrafts and artisans were not cared for and they collapsed. Jagirdars and Zamindars were unhappy when the English resumed the tax-free lands and confiscated jagirs.
• The Annexation of Oudh and disbanding of its army made the soldiers angry and the Zamindars and Taluqdars were disturbed when their lands were annexed. This led to unemployment and starvation.
• Soon the educated Indians were jobless too as only the English got high posts and good salaries.
• Lack of attention to trade and industry led to their decline. Poverty and famine were common. All this turned the people against the English.

IV. Military Causes
• Indian sepoys had aided the British in setting up their Empire in India but they never got their due. They were ill-paid and ill-treated. The General Service Enlistment Act forced Indian sepoys to be sent to fight even on an overseas front.
• When the English lost the Afghan War, the Indian sepoys thought that the English were not invincible. As they were more in number compared to the English soldiers, they grew confident of revolting and winning.
• As the English soldiers went to fight the Crimean War they were less in number and weak in the British Indian Army. The rumour that the hundred years old British rule would soon end also encouraged soldiers.

V. The Immediate Cause : The Greased Cartridges
• The Enfield Rifle introduced in the army used greased cartridges. A rumour spread that cow and pig fat was used to grease the cartridges. This angered the Hindu as well as the Muslim sepoys. Mangal Pandey in Barrackpore, Bengal, refused to use the greased cartridges on 29 March, 1857. He killed two English officers. Further opposition led to uprisings in Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, etc.
• As a result many sepoys were dismissed. To avenge this, the sepoys killed officers or set their houses on fire. But with the aid of some loyal soldiers and Indian chiefs the British were able to suppress the Uprising of 1857 which is often called the ‘First War of Independence’.

VI. Results of the Great Uprising
• The Company’s Rule ended and the designation of the Governor-General changed. The Governor-General now came to be known as the Viceroy. The British Government was forced to accept previous treaties.

• Indian Princes were appeased by the English for aiding in suppression of the Great Uprising. The Peshwaship and Mughal Line were abolished, followed by the policy of ‘Divide and Rule’. With the rise of nationalism the English increased expenditure on the army.
• European troops were increased as troops of the Crown and the Company combined. Artillery was in European hands and different classes of sepoys were mixed.
• Promises of religious freedom and of free and impartial admittance to service were made. A strained relationship developed between the Indians and the Europeans as the English widened the gulf between the Hindus and the Muslims. Moreover, economic exploitation was on the rise.
• None of the promises made after suppression of the Great Uprising were kept by the British Crown. The nature of British imperialism underwent little change after it passed from the Company to the Crown.

ICSE History and Civics – X

Ans. (a) The Doctrine of Lapse was a policy implemented by the British East India Company under Governor-General Lord Dalhousie. The queen who became a victim of this policy was Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi. It allowed the British to annex any princely state where the ruler died without a natural heir. Adopted sons were not recognised as legitimate successors. This policy was used to expand British territories in India.

(b) Three economic causes of the Revolt of 1857:
(i) Heavy Taxation: The British imposed high land revenue and taxes, causing widespread distress among farmers and landowners.  
(ii) Destruction of Traditional Industries: The influx of British manufactured goods led to the decline of Indian handicrafts and traditional industries, causing unemployment and economic hardship.  
(iii) Subordination of Indian Industry: After the Industrial Revolution, England developed its textile industry and imposed high import duties on Indian goods while purchasing raw materials cheaply from India. This turned India into an economic colony, supplying raw materials and importing British-made goods. This led to India’s resource exploitation.

(c) Four administrative changes made by British after the Revolt are:
(i) Abolition of the East India Company: The British government dissolved the East India Company and took direct control of India.  
(ii) Establishment of the British Raj: The governance of India was transferred to the British Crown, and India was administered directly under the British Raj.  
(iii) Creation of the Secretary of State for India: A new position, the Secretary of State for India, was created to oversee Indian affairs, with an advisory council to assist.  
(iv) Reorganisation of the Army: The British reorganised the Indian army to ensure greater loyalty and prevent future revolts. They increased the ratio of British to Indian soldiers and restricted Indian access to key artillery and higher positions.

6. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 was a major uprising against the rule of the British East India Company, which functioned as a sovereign power on behalf of the British Crown. With reference to this, answer the following questions:

(a) How did the failure of this Revolt impact the Mughals and the Peshwas?  
[Application]

Ans. (a) The failure of the Revolt of 1857 had significant impacts on the Mughals and the Peshwas:
(i) End of Mughal Rule: The British formally ended the Mughal Empire. The last Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah II, was captured, tried, and exiled to Rangoon, Burma.  
(ii) Abolition of the Mughal Title: The Mughal title and any remaining authority associated with the Mughal dynasty were abolished, marking the complete end of Mughal political influence in India.  
(iii) End of the Peshwa Line: The title of Peshwa was abolished, and Nana Sahib, the last Peshwa, was declared a fugitive by the British for his role in the revolt.  
(iv) Confiscation of Estates: The properties and estates of the Peshwas were confiscated by the British, and their political power was dismantled permanently.

(b) Mention any four changes made in the army after the Revolt.  
[Analysis] [ICSE SQP 2024, 2025]

Ans. (b) After the Revolt of 1857, the British made several changes in the Indian army to ensure better control and prevent future rebellions. Four key changes were:
(i) Increase in the Ratio of British to Indian Soldiers: The number of British soldiers was increased relative to Indian soldiers to maintain tighter control and ensure loyalty.  
(ii) Reorganisation of Units: The army was reorganised so that Indian regiments were mixed, preventing soldiers from the same region or caste from serving together to reduce the chances of collective mutiny.  
(iii) Restriction on Indian Access to Artillery and Key Positions: Indians were largely excluded from artillery units and key command positions, which were reserved for British officers to limit Indian influence and authority within the military.  
(iv) Recruitment Policy Changes: Recruitment policies were altered to favour martial races deemed loyal to the British, such as Sikhs, Gurkhas, and Pathans, while discouraging recruitment from areas involved in the rebellion.
